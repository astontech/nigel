# What the interviewer is doing

Drawn from guidance written by interviewers for interviewers: Amazon bar raisers, hiring-manager guides at Ashby and Metaview, Joel Spolsky, Jacob Kaplan-Moss, interviewing.io's Amazon dataset, and two staffing-firm interviewer guides. They converge. Sources are listed at the end; a point resting on one source is marked *(single source)*.

## The mechanic

The opening monologue is a table of contents. The interviewer picks one line from it and spends ten to fifteen minutes pulling that thread. A memorized opener collapses at the first genuine follow-up; the candidate who never practiced out loud rambles. Both fail. The thing to prepare is the **material under the script** — the fact sheet — so any thread can be pulled. The script is downstream of it.

## What they are trying to establish

| Question in their head | How they get at it |
|---|---|
| Is the resume true? | Ask about a listed project and see whether the candidate can elaborate. Inability to go deeper on their own resume is the first red flag. |
| Did you decide, or execute? | "What did *you* specifically do?" until they can separate the candidate's contribution from the team's momentum. |
| How is your judgment? | Why this over that, what was rejected, what it cost. Reasoning scores; technology names do not. |
| Did it matter? | Why the work was prioritized, who benefited, what the result was. Answers should end on a result. |
| Can you explain it plainly? | They ask the candidate to drop the jargon. Candidates who cannot are rejected. |
| Do you care about the work? | Whether the candidate gets animated. Older advice, still widely repeated. |

## Signals that separate "did the work" from "was nearby"

- **Detail survives the third follow-up.** Real experience has near-infinite detail; embellished experience runs out around the third "why" or "how." Interviewers treat this as the one reliable signal.
- **Decision questions, not just description questions.** Owners answer "why this over the alternative" and "what would you change" without hesitating. Bystanders describe.
- **Numbers and timeline.** Dates, volumes, team size, the candidate's slice versus the team's.
- **A precise "I" / "we" boundary.** Not the pronoun count — the precision. Reflexive "we" reads as hiding; overselling reads worse.
- **Owns a failure and a lesson.** Blame, or a missing lesson, is a red flag.

## Candidate failure modes interviewers name

- Vague role ("I was involved in"), no quantification ("delivered very quickly").
- Technology name-dropping instead of reasoning; complexity presented as achievement.
- Monologuing; a memorized STAR script; buzzword fluff.
- Getting defensive when a decision is questioned.
- Blaming teammates or managers; speaking badly of a past client.
- Cannot explain plainly when asked.
- Picked a project they did not drive. *(single source: Ashby also marks migration and coordination projects as weak choices — frame such work by the decisions made inside it.)*
- Improvising instead of saying "I don't know."
- Answering a question the interviewer did not ask.

## When the client is interviewing a contractor

Two staffing-firm interviewer guides, consistent with each other and the only sources found on this. A permanent hire is asked "will you grow here?" A contractor is asked "can you deliver this scope, starting now?" The client line manager has about an hour and is also listening for:

- **Ramp-up speed.** "What do you need on Monday to be useful by Friday?" "Walk me through your first two days in an unfamiliar codebase."
- **Low supervision.** Decisions made without being told to.
- **Clean exits.** "How did your last engagement end? Would they take you back?"
- **Handling disagreement with the client.** Expected answer: advise once, clearly, then execute their call.
- **Stack overlap.** Whether the experience justifies the rate on *their* stack.

## Rehearsal rules (what the candidate does with all this)

- Out loud, then interrogated. Read the openers aloud once. Then have someone pull one thread five questions deep. The second part is the rehearsal.
- A dialogue, not a speech. Finish a block, stop, let them steer.
- "I" for what you did, "we" for the team, and the line is crisp.
- Every line has to survive three follow-ups or it comes out.
- Explain, don't defend.
- "I don't know" beats improvising. Never speak badly of a past client or teammate.
